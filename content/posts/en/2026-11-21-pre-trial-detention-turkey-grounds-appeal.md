A Turkish court may order pre-trial detention (tutuklama) only if concrete evidence shows strong suspicion of an offence and there is a statutory ground for detention (Article 100(1) of the Turkish Code of Criminal Procedure, Ceza Muhakemesi Kanunu No. 5271, CMK). Even then, detention is not allowed if it is disproportionate to the seriousness of the case and the expected sentence. You can object (itiraz) to a detention order within **two weeks** (Articles 101(5) and 268(1) CMK).

Searches for “tutuklama” rose in Turkey this week alongside news about fraud investigations. This article is not about any particular case; it explains the legal framework. According to the CMK text on mevzuat.gov.tr as of November 2026, the latest amendment to the detention rules is point (k) added to Article 100(3) by Law No. 7528 (in force since 18 October 2024). Law No. 7499 extended the objection period from seven days to two weeks (in force since 1 June 2024). Law No. 7589 (the 12th Judicial Package, 31 July 2026) amended Articles 80, 231, 247 and 308 CMK but did not touch the detention provisions.

## Grounds for detention under Article 100 CMK

Article 100(1) and (2) CMK (unofficial translation):

> Where there is concrete evidence showing strong suspicion of an offence and a ground for detention, a detention order may be issued against the suspect or the accused. A detention order may not be issued if it is disproportionate to the significance of the case or to the expected penalty or security measure.
>
> A ground for detention may be assumed in the following cases:
>
> a) if there are concrete facts giving rise to the suspicion that the suspect or accused will flee, has fled or is hiding;
>
> b) if the conduct of the suspect or accused gives rise to strong suspicion that he or she will 1. destroy, hide or alter evidence, or 2. attempt to put pressure on witnesses, victims or others.

Turkish original:

> Kuvvetli suç şüphesinin varlığını gösteren somut delillerin ve bir tutuklama nedeninin bulunması halinde, şüpheli veya sanık hakkında tutuklama kararı verilebilir. İşin önemi, verilmesi beklenen ceza veya güvenlik tedbiri ile ölçülü olmaması halinde, tutuklama kararı verilemez.
>
> Aşağıdaki hallerde bir tutuklama nedeni var sayılabilir:
>
> a) Şüpheli veya sanığın kaçması, saklanması veya kaçacağı şüphesini uyandıran somut olgular varsa.
>
> b) Şüpheli veya sanığın davranışları;
>
> 1. Delilleri yok etme, gizleme veya değiştirme,
>
> 2. Tanık, mağdur veya başkaları üzerinde baskı yapılması girişiminde bulunma,
>
> Hususlarında kuvvetli şüphe oluşturuyorsa.

So there are two grounds: risk of flight and risk of tampering with evidence. Both must rest on concrete facts or on the person’s conduct.

Paragraph 3 lists “catalogue offences”. For these, a ground for detention may be assumed once there is strong suspicion based on concrete evidence. The list includes intentional killing, theft including its aggravated forms, robbery, drug trafficking, sexual assault, sexual abuse of children, setting up a criminal organisation, and intentional injury against women or against teachers or health workers on duty. Fraud is not on the list.

Paragraph 4 sets out a ban: detention is not allowed for offences punishable only by a judicial fine, or for offences with a maximum prison term of two years or less, except intentional offences against bodily integrity.

## Catalogue offences, flight risk and the 2026 Constitutional Court ruling

In a judgment of 4 March 2026, application no. 2024/31038 (Official Gazette of 16 October 2026, No. 33402), the First Section of the Turkish Constitutional Court (Anayasa Mahkemesi, AYM) reviewed a detention order for aggravated fraud. The trial court had relied on the offence being a catalogue offence and on the expected sentence.

The Court found:

- Aggravated fraud is not a catalogue offence under Article 100(3) CMK, so the trial court could not infer a risk of flight or tampering from the type of offence alone.
- After the applicant gave his statement during the investigation, no measure at all, not even judicial control, had been imposed, and he attended the first hearing voluntarily while at liberty. A flight risk cannot be inferred merely from the possible sentence.
- Because the reasons making detention necessary were not shown, the right to personal liberty and security (Article 19(3) of the Constitution) was violated. The applicant was awarded a net TRY 100,000 for non-pecuniary damage.

The judgment refers to European Court of Human Rights rulings against Turkey that found a wrong classification as a catalogue offence to breach Article 5(1) of the Convention. It concerns one case, but it shows which points can be raised in an objection to detention.

## Who orders detention?

During the investigation, the **criminal judgeship of peace** (sulh ceza hâkimi) decides at the prosecutor’s request; during trial, the **court** decides at the prosecutor’s request or of its own motion (Article 101(1) CMK). The prosecutor must also explain why judicial control (adli kontrol) would be insufficient.

Decisions ordering or extending detention, or rejecting a release request, must give concrete reasons for the strong suspicion, the ground for detention, proportionality and why judicial control is insufficient (Article 101(2)). The person is entitled to a defence lawyer of their choice or one appointed by the bar (Article 101(3)). If no detention order is made, the person is released at once (Article 101(4)).

On the judge’s instruction, a relative or a person named by the detainee is informed without delay of the order and of every extension (Article 107(1)). If the person is a foreign national, their consulate is informed unless they object in writing (Article 107(3)).

## How long can pre-trial detention last?

Article 102 CMK sets the upper limits:

- **Cases outside the jurisdiction of the high criminal court (ağır ceza mahkemesi):** one year at most, extendable by six months in compelling cases with reasons.
- **High criminal court cases:** two years at most; extensions may not exceed three years in total. For offences in Book Two, Part Four, Chapters 4 to 7 of the Turkish Criminal Code (TCK) and offences under the Anti-Terror Law, extensions may not exceed five years.
- **Investigation stage:** six months, or one year in high criminal court cases. For the state security and terrorism offences above and for offences committed collectively, at most eighteen months, extendable by a further six months with reasons (Article 102(4)).

For children under 15 at the time of the offence these limits are halved; for children under 18 they apply at three quarters (Article 102(5)).

These are ceilings, not the expected length. During the investigation the judge reviews whether detention must continue at least **every 30 days**, after hearing the suspect or their lawyer (Article 108(1)). During trial the court reviews it at every hearing (Article 108(3)).

## Objecting to detention and asking for release

There are two routes.

**Objection (itiraz).** An objection lies against the detention order (Article 101(5)). It must be filed within **two weeks** of learning of the decision, by petition to the authority that made it or by a statement recorded by the court clerk (Article 268(1)). If that authority does not accept it, it must send the objection within three days at most to the reviewing authority (Article 268(2)). For decisions of a criminal judgeship of peace, where there are several in the same place, this is the next judgeship by number (Article 268(3)(a)). The objection is normally decided without a hearing and “as soon as possible” (Article 271(1) and (3)). The reviewing authority’s decision is final, but if that authority is the first to order detention, an objection lies against it (Article 271(4)).

**Request for release.** The suspect or accused may ask to be released at any stage (Article 104(1)). The decision must be made within three days, or seven days for offences committed in the context of an organisation (Article 105(1)). These decisions can also be objected to.

## Judicial control instead of detention

Even where a ground for detention exists, the judge may order judicial control instead (Article 109(1)). It is also available where detention is banned (Article 109(2)). The obligations are listed in Article 109(3), for example:

- not leaving the country (travel ban),
- reporting regularly to a designated place,
- paying a security,
- not leaving one’s home or a particular area,
- not going to specified places.

Judicial control may also be ordered instead of detention for a suspect who, because of serious illness or disability, cannot live alone in prison, and for a woman who is pregnant or gave birth less than six months ago (Article 109(4)).

Time spent under judicial control is in principle not deducted from the sentence. Under house arrest, every two days count as one day (Article 109(6)). Judicial control may also be imposed on someone released because the maximum detention period expired (Article 109(7)).

## If you live in Germany or the Netherlands

The CMK has no rule making residence abroad a ground for detention in itself; flight risk must rest on “concrete facts” under Article 100(2)(a). Whether residence abroad counts as such a fact is not expressly regulated. In the author’s assessment, this has to be weighed in each case together with how the person has acted in the proceedings, for example whether they came to their interview and hearing on their own; the Constitutional Court judgment above also gave weight to that conduct.

In practice, a **travel ban** under judicial control (Article 109(3)(a)) is more common than detention. You then cannot return to Germany or the Netherlands until it is lifted. An objection lies against decisions on judicial control (Article 111(2)).

A person who is released must give their address and any telephone number and report every change until the proceedings end; otherwise documents are served at the old address (Article 106). If your address abroad changes, tell the court.

If a relative of yours is detained in Turkey, the detention order and extensions are communicated to relatives (Article 107(1)). Notifying the consulate (Article 107(3)) applies to foreign nationals; the CMK has no separate rule for dual nationals who also hold Turkish citizenship.

## Compensation for unlawful detention

A person detained, or kept in detention, outside the conditions set by law may claim pecuniary and non-pecuniary damages from the State (Article 141(1)(a) CMK). So may a person lawfully detained who is later acquitted or whose case is dropped (Article 141(1)(e)). The claim must be made within three months of being notified that the decision became final, and in any event within one year of that date (Article 142(1)). Since Law No. 7499, claims under points (e), (f) and (l) go to the Compensation Commission under Law No. 6384; the others are decided by the high criminal court where the claimant lives (Article 142(2)).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the deadline to object to pre-trial detention in Turkey?

Two weeks from learning of the decision (Article 268(1) CMK, since 1 June 2024 under Law No. 7499).

### How long can pre-trial detention last in Turkey?

Outside the high criminal court, one year plus six months; in high criminal court cases two years plus up to three years of extensions, and up to five years for certain state security and terrorism offences. Shorter limits apply during the investigation (Article 102(4)).

### Can living abroad lead to detention in Turkey?

The law does not list residence abroad as a ground; flight risk requires concrete facts (Article 100(2)(a)). A travel ban is more common (Article 109(3)(a)).
