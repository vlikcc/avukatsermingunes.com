Article 3 of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721 (Türk Medenî Kanunu, TMK) deals with good faith (iyiniyet). Where the law attaches a legal effect to good faith, good faith is presumed; but a person who fails to take the care the circumstances require cannot claim it. In practice you will meet this provision mostly in title deed cancellation cases (tapu iptali ve tescil davası): if your property in Turkey changed hands without your knowledge, or you bought property there yourself, the dispute often turns on Article 3 together with Article 1023 TMK.

## Article 3 TMK text

Unofficial translation:

> **II. Good faith**
>
> Article 3 – Where the law attaches a legal effect to good faith, the existence of good faith is presumed.
>
> However, a person who has not exercised the care that the circumstances required of them cannot claim good faith.

Turkish original:

> **II. İyiniyet**
>
> Madde 3- Kanunun iyiniyete hukukî bir sonuç bağladığı durumlarda, asıl olan iyiniyetin varlığıdır.
>
> Ancak, durumun gereklerine göre kendisinden beklenen özeni göstermeyen kimse iyiniyet iddiasında bulunamaz.

The article sits in the introductory part of the Code under “B. Scope of legal relations” and has not changed since 1 January 2002. As of November 2026, the amendment table of the current text in the Ministry of Justice’s UYAP Mevzuat system (recorded 31 July 2026) does not list Article 3; the last law in the table is Law No. 7589, which amended Articles 440 and 444 (those changes in force since 31 October 2026). Articles 158, 712, 988–991, 1007, 1020 and 1023–1025, mentioned below, are not listed either.

## Legislative reasoning

According to the official reasoning, the provision corresponds to Article 3 of the former Civil Code No. 743. The heading became “Good faith”, and the effect of good faith was not limited to the creation of rights but extended to every case in which the law attaches a legal effect to it. Paragraph 1 was aligned with the German text of Article 3 of the Swiss Civil Code (source: reasoning of the government bill submitted to the Grand National Assembly).

## Explanation

### Article 3 works only alongside another provision

Article 3 does not create a right on its own. It applies where a specific provision protects the person acting in good faith. Examples in the Civil Code:

- Article 1023: a third party who acquires ownership or another right in rem relying in good faith on an entry in the land register is protected in that acquisition.
- Articles 988–991: for movable property, a person who acquires in good faith from someone the item was entrusted to is protected; against an acquirer not in good faith, the previous possessor can sue at any time.
- Article 712: a person registered as owner without a valid legal ground who possesses the property for ten years, undisputed, without interruption and in good faith, can no longer have that ownership challenged.
- Article 158: if a marriage is declared void, the spouse who was in good faith when marrying keeps the personal status acquired through the marriage.

### Burden of proof: an exception to Article 6

Under Article 6 TMK, unless the law provides otherwise, each party must prove the facts on which it bases its right. Article 3(1) is such a provision. In the 2026 decision listed below, the Assembly of Civil Chambers of the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu) said that Article 1023 is an application of the general rule in Article 3, and that the claimant must prove the bad faith of the person who relied on the register.

### Where paragraph 2 cuts the presumption short

The same decision notes that bad faith is hard to prove, so the claimant may rely on factual presumptions. In Court of Cassation case law these include the property changing hands very often within short intervals, or a price far below market value. Payment of the price alone does not settle the matter, according to the Assembly; the question is whether the buyer knew of the defect, or could have known it by exercising the care expected of them. This matches Article 1024(1), which refers to a third party who “knows or ought to know”.

The land register is public, and no one can claim to be unaware of an entry in it (Article 1020). Relying on the register, however, concerns not the entry as such but the validity of the transaction behind it; Article 3(2) sets the limit of that reliance.

## What this means in practice if you live abroad

**If your property was sold without your knowledge.** A registration based on a forged or altered power of attorney (vekâletname), or on an agent exceeding their authority, is unlawful (Article 1024(2)), and you can sue to have the register corrected (Article 1025(1)). If the property has meanwhile passed to a third party in good faith, that person’s acquisition is protected (Article 1023, Article 1025(2)). In the decisions below this worked as follows: the first acquirer relying on a forged power of attorney was not protected; later buyers were protected unless their bad faith was proven. If you cannot recover the property, claims for its value or for damages against the agent and those acting with them come into play. The State is also liable for all damage resulting from keeping the land register; such claims are heard by the court where the register is kept (Article 1007).

**When you grant a power of attorney.** Naming the property and the share to be sold precisely makes it easier to notice when someone goes beyond those limits. In the 1st Civil Chamber decision below, the crossing out of “50%” was found to be noticeable at first glance, and the buyer was not protected. For the contents of the power of attorney and the consulate procedure: [Selling property in Turkey from abroad: POA and deed fees](/en/publications/selling-property-in-turkey-from-abroad).

**When you buy in Turkey.** The seller’s name on the title deed is usually enough, but not always. In the author’s assessment: if the sale is made through an agent, ask to see the power of attorney; check whether the price is close to market value and how often the property has changed hands recently; and pay through a bank. This reduces the risk of later facing the objection that you did not take due care. As the Assembly’s decision shows, the answer depends on the facts; in the same case, members of the Assembly reached different conclusions.

Ownership and other rights in rem over immovable property are governed by the law of the country where the property is located at the time of the transaction (Article 21(1) of Turkey’s Private International Law Act No. 5718, MÖHUK). For property in Turkey that is Turkish law, so living in Germany, the Netherlands or the UK does not change how Articles 3 and 1023 TMK apply. Questions about German or Dutch land registry law are outside this article; for those, please consult a lawyer admitted in that country.

## Court of Cassation decisions

**Yargıtay (Court of Cassation), Assembly of Civil Chambers, E. 2025/399, K. 2026/379, 18.06.2026**

The municipal council decision on which the sale was based had been annulled by the administrative court, making the first buyer’s registration unlawful. The property then changed hands at short intervals, at low prices stated in the deeds, through powers of attorney held by the same estate agent or people linked to him. The Assembly held that Article 3 presumes good faith and that the claimant must prove bad faith; but, citing paragraph 2 (“a person who has not exercised the care the circumstances required cannot claim good faith”), it found that the later buyers could not rely on Article 1023 and upheld the cancellation of the title. The decision was by majority; the dissenting opinions considered bad faith unproven.

**Yargıtay, 1st Civil Chamber, E. 2026/4458, K. 2026/5056, 22.06.2026**

The claimant had given a power of attorney to sell half of his share; the words “50%” were crossed out and the whole share was sold. The first-instance court and the regional appeal court found that the alteration was noticeable at first glance and that the buyer, as the first acquirer relying on a forged power of attorney, could not rely on Article 1023. The Chamber upheld this with brief reasons.

**Yargıtay, 1st Civil Chamber, E. 2026/6267, K. 2026/6443, 24.09.2026**

The claimant had given a relative a power of attorney to sell a single plot; a substitute agent sold other plots as well. Following the Chamber’s earlier reversal quoted in the decision (E. 2022/3341, K. 2022/6617), the claim for cancellation of title was dismissed because the last registered owners were in good faith and protected by Article 1023; the agent and the defendant proven to have acted in concert with him were held liable for the alternative claim for the property’s value. The Chamber upheld the judgment given after that reversal.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does Article 3 of the Turkish Civil Code say?

That good faith is presumed where the law attaches a legal effect to it, and that a person who did not take the care the circumstances required cannot claim good faith.

### Can the presumption of good faith be rebutted?

Yes. The other side can rebut it by proving that the acquirer knew or ought to have known of the defect. Under paragraph 2, a person who did not take due care cannot claim good faith in the first place.

### Can I get back property sold with a forged power of attorney?

The registration is unlawful and you can sue to correct the register. If the property has passed to a third party in good faith, that acquisition is protected (Article 1023 TMK); damages and the State’s liability under Article 1007 TMK then become relevant.
