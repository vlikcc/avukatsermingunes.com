Once a Turkish court declares a missing person absent (gaiplik kararı), every right that depends on that person's death can be exercised as if the death had been proved. The declaration takes effect not from the date of the judgment but from the day of the mortal danger or the day the last news was received. This is Article 35 of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721 (Türk Medenî Kanunu, TMK). Its effects show up mainly in the inheritance, the marriage and the civil registry, so families who obtain a declaration in Türkiye for a relative who went missing abroad deal with this article directly.

The conditions, who may apply and which court decides are covered in [Article 32 TMK – Declaration of absence in Turkey](/en/publications/legislation/article-32-tmk-declaration-of-absence-turkey). This article looks at what happens after the judgment.

## Article 35 TMK – text

Unofficial translation:

> **4. Effect**
>
> Article 35 – If the public notice produces no result, the court declares the person absent, and rights dependent on death are exercised exactly as if the death of the absent person had been proved.
>
> The declaration of absence takes effect from the day on which the mortal danger occurred or the last news was received.

Turkish original:

> **4. Hükmü**
>
> Madde 35- İlândan sonuç alınamazsa, mahkeme gaipliğe karar verir ve ölüme bağlı haklar, aynen gaibin ölümü ispatlanmış gibi kullanılır.
>
> Gaiplik kararı ölüm tehlikesinin gerçekleştiği veya son haberin alındığı günden başlayarak hüküm doğurur.

As of November 2026 the current TMK text on mevzuat.gov.tr carries no amendment note for Article 35. The last law in the amendment table is Law No. 7589 (Articles 440 and 444, in force since 31 October 2026), which does not touch Article 35.

## Legislative reasoning

According to the reasoning, the article corresponds to Article 34 of the former Civil Code and was taken over unchanged in substance, with simplified wording. The old phrase "tıpkı gaibin ölümü tebeyyün etmiş gibi" became "aynen gaibin ölümü ispatlanmış gibi"; both mean "as if the death of the absent person had been established". (Source: reasoning of the government bill submitted to the Grand National Assembly.)

## Explanation

### The retroactive effect

The judgment reaches back to the day of the mortal danger or of the last news (Article 35(2) TMK). To be an heir, a person must be alive and capable of inheriting when the deceased dies (Article 580(1) TMK). Read together, the heirs of the absent person are determined as of that earlier date.

An example: the last news came in 2015, the absent person's mother died in 2019, and the declaration was made in 2022. The mother was alive in 2015, so she is an heir of the absent person. Her death in 2019 does not change that; her share passes to her own heirs (Article 580(2) TMK).

The reverse situation has its own rule. If an heir is missing when an estate opens and it cannot be proved that they were alive at that moment, their share is placed under official administration (Article 586(1) TMK). Those who would receive the share if the missing heir is not alive can ask for a declaration of absence and for the share to be handed over to them (Article 586(2) TMK). If an inheritance falls to the absent person after their heirs have already received the estate, no new declaration of absence is needed for it (Article 587 TMK).

The retroactive effect also matters for parentage. If the husband has been declared absent, the three-hundred-day period of the presumption of paternity runs from the day of the mortal danger or the last news (Article 285(3) TMK).

### Security from the heirs

The heirs and others entitled to the estate must provide security before the estate assets are handed over to them. The security covers their duty to return the assets to anyone with a better right who appears later, or to the absent person (Article 584(1) TMK). Periods and starting points (Article 584(2) and (3) TMK):

- disappearance in mortal danger: five years, counted from the handover of the estate,
- no news for a long time: fifteen years, counted from the date of the last news,
- in any case no longer than until the absent person would have turned one hundred.

Because the fifteen years run from the last news, part of that period may already have passed by the time the judgment is given. The article does not say what form the security must take.

### If the absent person returns

If the absent person reappears, or others prove a better right, those who received the estate must return it under the rules on possession (Article 585(1) TMK). These rules distinguish between a possessor in good faith and one in bad faith:

- A possessor in good faith owes no compensation for proper use and is not liable for loss or damage (Article 993 TMK). They may claim necessary and useful expenses (Article 994 TMK).
- A possessor in bad faith must compensate the damage caused by wrongfully keeping the property and the value of the fruits gathered or neglected (Article 995 TMK).

The duty of good-faith recipients to return assets to those with a better right is subject to the limitation period of the action for recovery of an inheritance (miras sebebiyle istihkak davası; Articles 585(2) and 639 TMK). Article 585 sets no separate period for the duty towards the absent person themselves. Whether that duty is limited in time is disputed; there is no express statutory rule and no binding court decision on it. Which view a court would take in a particular case is uncertain.

If the declaration is set aside, the registry reflects it: the change is written in the remarks column of the entry (Article 46 TMK), and the closed record is reopened (Article 14(2) of the Civil Registration Services Law No. 5490, Nüfus Hizmetleri Kanunu).

### No one comes forward: the Treasury

If the assets of a person whose survival is unknown, or a share of an inheritance that fell to them, have been under official administration for ten years, or if the time has passed in which they would have turned one hundred, the court declares them absent at the request of the Treasury (Hazine). If no one entitled appears during the notice period, the estate passes to the State unless the law provides otherwise. The State must return it to the absent person or to those with a better right, just like any other recipient (Article 588 TMK).

### Marriage and the spouse

A declaration of absence does not end the marriage by itself; the spouse cannot remarry until the court dissolves the marriage (evliliğin feshi, Article 131 TMK). The spouse's statutory inheritance right, on the other hand, depends on the death. So even while the marriage has not been dissolved, the spouse is among the heirs of the absent person, with the share set out in Article 499 TMK.

After the marriage is dissolved, the waiting period in Article 132 TMK applies to the woman: she may not marry until three hundred days have passed from the end of the marriage. The period ends with a birth. If it is established that she is not pregnant from the previous marriage, the court lifts the period.

### Entry in the civil registry

The declaration is entered in the death register on notification by the judge (Article 45 TMK). Under the Civil Registration Services Law, the court notifies the local registry office (nüfus müdürlüğü) within ten days, and the declaration is entered in the family register (aile kütüğü) (Article 34 of Law No. 5490). Absence is one of the grounds on which a registry record is closed (Article 14(1) of Law No. 5490).

### Land registered in the absent person's name

If land is registered in the name of a person declared absent twenty years ago, the possessor may ask for ownership to be registered in their own name, provided they have held the land as owner for twenty years without interruption and without being sued (Article 713(2) TMK). This can become relevant when land of the absent person is left for a long time without being transferred to the heirs.

## What this means in practice if you live abroad

Turkish law governs the declaration of absence of a Turkish citizen ([Article 11 MÖHUK – Declaration of absence](/en/publications/legislation/article-11-mohuk-declaration-of-absence)). The retroactive effect of Article 35 therefore decides matters for the estate in Türkiye even if the person went missing in Germany or the Netherlands. When the list of heirs is drawn up, the question is who was alive on the date of the last news; a family member who died in Germany after that date can still be an heir of the absent person. Whoever claims that someone was alive at a particular time must prove it (Article 29(1) TMK), so that family member's death certificate showing the date of death matters.

The security is required for the handover of estate assets in Türkiye. If the absent person has land or bank accounts there, the heirs should factor in this obligation. For proving heirship after the declaration, see [Certificate of inheritance in Turkey: notary or court?](/en/publications/certificate-of-inheritance-turkey).

If a court in Germany or the Netherlands has already issued a decision about the missing person, for example a declaration of death, entering it in the Turkish registry is a question of recognition (see the Article 11 MÖHUK post). For the procedure in Germany or the Netherlands, please contact the competent authorities or a lawyer admitted in that country.

## Frequently asked questions

### From when does a declaration of absence take effect in Turkey?

From the day of the mortal danger or the last news (Article 35(2) TMK). The heirs are determined as of that day.

### Can the heirs take the estate straight away?

Only against security, for five or fifteen years and at most until the absent person would have turned one hundred (Article 584 TMK).

### What happens if the absent person comes back?

The heirs return what they received under the rules on possession (Article 585(1) TMK). A possessor in good faith may claim necessary and useful expenses (Article 994 TMK).

### Does a declaration of absence end the marriage?

No. The court must dissolve the marriage before the spouse can remarry (Article 131(1) TMK). After that, a three-hundred-day waiting period applies to the woman (Article 132 TMK).
