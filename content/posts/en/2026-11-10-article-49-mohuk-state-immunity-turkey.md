Article 49 of Turkish Law No. 5718 on Private International Law and International Civil Procedure (MÖHUK) sets out when a foreign state cannot rely on immunity from jurisdiction (yargı muafiyeti) before Turkish courts: in disputes arising from private-law relationships, it can be sued in Turkey. The provision matters to local staff of foreign embassies and consulates in Turkey, to landlords who let property to an embassy, and to anyone injured in an accident involving an embassy vehicle.

## Article 49 MÖHUK – text

Unofficial translation:

> **Cases in which a foreign state does not enjoy immunity from jurisdiction**
>
> Article 49 – (1) A foreign state shall not be granted immunity from jurisdiction in legal disputes arising from private-law relationships.
>
> (2) In such disputes, service may be made on the diplomatic representatives of the foreign state.

Turkish original:

> **Yabancı devletin yargı muafiyetinden yararlanamayacağı hâller**
>
> MADDE 49 – (1) Yabancı devlete, özel hukuk ilişkilerinden doğan hukukî uyuşmazlıklarda yargı muafiyeti tanınmaz.
>
> (2) Bu gibi uyuşmazlıklarda yabancı devletin diplomatik temsilcilerine tebligat yapılabilir.

As of November 2026, the current text in the UYAP Mevzuat system (recorded 5 June 2025) carries no amendment note for this article; the law's list of amendments shows only the change to Article 27 made by Law No. 7550.

## Legislative reasoning

According to the reasoning of the draft law, the article reproduces Article 33 of the former Law No. 2675 word for word. The rule has therefore been the same since 1982, and decisions of the Yargıtay (Court of Cassation) under the old law remain relevant.

## Explanation

The article follows restrictive immunity. A foreign state cannot be sued in a Turkish court over acts done in the exercise of sovereign authority. In disputes arising from relationships any private person could enter into, such as a lease, an employment contract or a traffic accident, it cannot claim immunity. The law does not define what counts as a sovereign act; the court decides on the facts of the case.

Article 49 concerns the state itself. The personal immunity of diplomats is a separate rule. In a case about an accident with an embassy car, the Yargıtay upheld the stay of proceedings against the diplomat who was driving, under Article 31 of the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, but held that the claim against the embassy, and so against the state it represents, had to proceed (see below).

Jurisdiction of the Turkish courts is a procedural requirement (Code of Civil Procedure, HMK Art. 114(1)(a)). Which Turkish court hears the case follows from Article 40 MÖHUK onwards ([Article 40 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-40-mohuk-international-jurisdiction-turkey)).

Paragraph 2 deals with service (tebligat). The Yargıtay set aside an enforcement court's order for service directly on the diplomatic representative. Under Article 45 of the Regulation implementing the Notification Law, a document that cannot be served on its addressee in Turkey is returned, and the authority that issued it forwards it through its ministry to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The Notification Law No. 7201 has no separate provision on service on foreign states.

Suing a state and enforcing against it are different matters. Under a paragraph added in 2012 to Article 32 of the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (İİK), the enforcement order in a judgment-based enforcement against a foreign state must state separately that, subject to international treaties, enforcement may be levied on property belonging to the debtor state. Article 88 İİK provides that vehicles carrying a foreign head of state, speaker of parliament, head of government or government members cannot be taken into custody while those persons are in Turkey. The law does not say which other property can be attached. This question is disputed. That property used for sovereign purposes, such as an embassy building, cannot be attached is a view put forward in legal writing; I have not found an express statutory rule or a binding court decision on it. How a court would decide in a given case is uncertain.

## Practical points for people living abroad

### Local staff of foreign missions in Turkey

If you work in Turkey as local staff at another state's embassy or consulate, your employment contract is a private-law relationship. The Yargıtay held, in a claim by a former employee of a foreign embassy to establish insured periods of service, that there was no immunity (see below). Which court has jurisdiction follows from Article 44 MÖHUK ([Article 44 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-44-mohuk-employment-disputes-jurisdiction)). Which duties are close enough to sovereign functions to attract immunity, and how far immunity reaches in claims such as reinstatement, is disputed. I have not found an express statutory rule or a binding decision on this; how a court would decide in a given case is uncertain.

### Staff of Turkish consulates in Germany

Article 49 concerns foreign states. The Republic of Türkiye cannot claim immunity before its own courts; the question there is which branch of the judiciary hears the case, and that depends on the employee's status. In a case reviewed by the Yargıtay (10th Civil Chamber, E. 2025/6844, K. 2026/3892, decided 06.04.2026), a former employee of a Turkish consulate abroad challenged deductions from their pay. The claim was dismissed as res judicata because an earlier claim with the same request had been dismissed on the ground that the administrative courts had jurisdiction; the Yargıtay upheld this. Whether Türkiye can be sued before German courts is a question of German law, and for that you need a lawyer admitted in Germany.

For other employment questions see [employment law](/en/practice-areas/employment-law).

## Yargıtay decisions

**Yargıtay 10th Civil Chamber (Hukuk Dairesi), E. 2010/15750, K. 2012/5611, decided 22.03.2012**

The labour court had dismissed a former foreign-embassy employee's claim to establish insured service because the workplace was an embassy. The Chamber reversed: no rule excludes Turkish citizens working at an embassy under an employment contract from insurance, and under Article 49 there was no immunity either.

**Yargıtay 4th Civil Chamber, E. 2010/6451, K. 2010/7394, decided 17.06.2010**

A damages claim after an accident with a car belonging to the Embassy of the Federal Republic of Germany. The Chamber upheld the stay against the diplomat who was driving, under Article 31 of the Vienna Convention. The embassy, however, represents the state, and under Article 33 of Law No. 2675 (now Article 49) there is no immunity in a private-law dispute; on that point the judgment was reversed.

**Yargıtay 4th Civil Chamber, E. 2022/1611, K. 2024/11631, decided 26.11.2024**

The first-instance court dismissed a damages claim against a foreign state over the cancellation of visas, holding that the visa decision was an exercise of sovereign authority and that Turkish courts lacked jurisdiction; the regional court of appeal rejected the appeal. The Yargıtay upheld the decision.

**Yargıtay 8th Civil Chamber, E. 2013/12683, K. 2013/19986, decided 26.12.2013**

In enforcement of a judgment against the German Embassy, the enforcement court had ordered service on the diplomatic representative. The Chamber reversed, referring to Article 45 of the Notification Regulation and the Ministry's circular on international service.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you sue an embassy in Turkey?

Yes, if the dispute arises from a private-law relationship such as a lease, an employment contract or a tort (Article 49(1) MÖHUK). For sovereign acts such as visa decisions, the state can claim immunity.

### Can you sue the diplomat personally?

The personal immunity of diplomats is a separate rule, and Article 49 does not remove it.

### Can a judgment be enforced against a foreign state's property?

Article 32 İİK provides for enforcement against property of a foreign state, but the law does not list which property can be attached, and the point is disputed.
