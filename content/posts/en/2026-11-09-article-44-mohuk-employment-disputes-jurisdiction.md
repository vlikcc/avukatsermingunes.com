Article 44 of Turkey’s Law No. 5718 on Private International Law and International Civil Procedure (MÖHUK) sets out when Turkish courts have international jurisdiction (milletlerarası yetki) over individual employment disputes with a foreign element. An employee suing the employer may also choose the Turkish court where the employer is domiciled, where the employee is domiciled, or where the employee habitually resides (mutad mesken). You may come across this article if you worked on a foreign construction site of a Turkish company, moved back to Turkey after years in Germany or the Netherlands, or work abroad for a company based in Turkey.

## Article 44 MÖHUK – text

Unofficial translation:

> **Actions arising from employment contracts and employment relationships**
>
> Article 44 – (1) In disputes arising from an individual employment contract or employment relationship, the court of the place in Turkey where the workplace in which the employee habitually carries out the work is located has jurisdiction. In actions brought by the employee against the employer, the Turkish courts of the employer’s domicile, the employee’s domicile or the employee’s habitual residence also have jurisdiction.

Original Turkish text:

> **İş sözleşmesi ve iş ilişkisi davaları**
>
> MADDE 44 – (1) Bireysel iş sözleşmesinden veya iş ilişkisinden doğan uyuşmazlıklarda işçinin işini mutaden yaptığı işyerinin Türkiye’de bulunduğu yer mahkemesi yetkilidir. İşçinin, işverene karşı açtığı davalarda işverenin yerleşim yeri, işçinin yerleşim yeri veya mutad meskeninin bulunduğu Türk mahkemeleri de yetkilidir.

The article has not changed since the law entered into force on 12 December 2007; as of November 2026, the current text in the UYAP Mevzuat system carries no amendment note for it.

## Legislative reasoning

According to the reasoning of the government bill, the former Law No. 2675 had no jurisdiction rule for employment contracts, and relying on the domestic venue rules was not enough. Following the case law of the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay) and legal writing, the basic rule became the court of the place where the employee habitually works. To protect the employee, claims against the employer may also be brought at the employer’s domicile, the employee’s domicile or the employee’s habitual residence. (Source: reasoning of the bill submitted to the Grand National Assembly, Article 43 of the bill; article numbers in the reasoning are those of the bill.)

## Explanation

As a rule, the international jurisdiction of Turkish courts follows the domestic rules on venue (Article 40 MÖHUK). For employment cases, that rule is Article 6 of the Labour Courts Law No. 7036 (İş Mahkemeleri Kanunu): the court of the defendant’s domicile when the action is filed, and the court of the place where the work was done. If the work was done abroad and the employer is also abroad, this rule points to no court in Turkey. Article 44 fills that gap in the employee’s favour.

The two sentences serve different parties. The first applies to both sides: if the employee habitually works at a workplace in Turkey, the court there has jurisdiction. The second applies only to claims by the employee against the employer and adds the three extra courts.

Jurisdiction and applicable law are separate questions. Even where a Turkish court has jurisdiction, the law governing the employment contract is determined under Article 27 MÖHUK, and that may be a foreign law ([Article 27 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-27-mohuk-employment-contracts)).

The jurisdiction of the courts listed in Article 44 cannot be excluded by agreement (Article 47(2) MÖHUK). A clause in your contract saying that “only the courts of country X” have jurisdiction does not stop you from suing in Turkey under Article 44. Jurisdiction agreements contrary to Article 6 of Law No. 7036 are also void (Article 6(5) of Law No. 7036).

## What matters if you live abroad

### Working on a foreign site of a Turkish employer

If you worked for a construction company based in Turkey on a site in Russia, Iraq or Africa, the Turkish labour court at the company’s seat has jurisdiction, because the employer is domiciled in Turkey. If you live in Turkey, you may also choose the labour court where you are domiciled. The Yargıtay has held the employee’s home court competent under Article 44 in such cases (see below).

### Moving back to Turkey from Germany or the Netherlands

If you worked for an employer in Germany or the Netherlands and have moved back to Turkey for good, the wording of the second sentence lets you sue at your Turkish home court even where the employer is foreign and all the work was done abroad. Without a choice of law, however, the Turkish court will in principle apply the law of the habitual workplace, so German or Dutch law (Article 27(2) MÖHUK); if the contract is more closely connected with another law, that law may apply (Article 27(4)). The judge applies foreign law of their own motion and may ask the parties for help in establishing its content (Article 2(1) MÖHUK). Whether a Turkish judgment will be recognised and enforced in Germany or the Netherlands depends on the law of that country; for that part, please consult a lawyer admitted there.

### A Turkish company with a workplace abroad

If you work permanently in Germany or the Netherlands for a company based in Turkey, the first sentence does not apply because your habitual workplace is not in Turkey. Since the employer is domiciled in Turkey, you can still sue it there (second sentence). Where the company has set up a separate local company abroad, you need to check who your employer actually is and where it is domiciled.

### Mandatory mediation before suing (dava şartı olarak arabuluculuk)

For claims by an employee or employer for receivables and compensation based on statute or on an individual or collective agreement, and for reinstatement claims (işe iade), applying to a mediator before filing the lawsuit is a procedural precondition (Article 3(1) of Law No. 7036). Compensation claims for work accidents and occupational diseases are excluded (Article 3(3)). From the application to the mediation office until the final report, limitation periods are suspended and time limits for forfeiture do not run (Article 3(17)). You may attend in person or through your lawyer (Article 3(18)).

Whether this precondition applies to disputes with a foreign element is disputed. Article 3 of Law No. 7036 makes no exception for international cases, and the Mediation Law No. 6325 expressly covers disputes with a foreign element (Article 1(2) of Law No. 6325). On the other hand, the competent mediation office is determined by the other party’s domicile or the place where the work was done (Article 3(5) of Law No. 7036); if both are abroad, the law does not say which office to apply to. I have not found a binding court decision that answers this question directly. In my assessment, a Turkish court will require the precondition, and the safe course is to apply for mediation before suing; how a court will decide in a particular case is not certain.

## Court of Cassation decisions

**Yargıtay 5. Hukuk Dairesi, E. 2025/8983, K. 2025/13604, T. 27.10.2025**

The employee first worked in Istanbul and then at stores in Kazakhstan and Russia, and later sued in Izmir, where the employee was domiciled. After the Izmir and Istanbul labour courts each declined jurisdiction, the Yargıtay designated the competent court. The chamber found that the employee had used the right of choice under Article 44 by suing at the court of domicile, and held the Izmir labour court competent. To the same effect in another case where the work was done abroad: Yargıtay 5. Hukuk Dairesi, E. 2025/5739, K. 2025/8746, T. 16.06.2025.

**Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2025/5, K. 2026/59, T. 11.02.2026**

An employee who had worked on a Turkish company’s sites in Kosovo, Russia and other countries sued in Turkey for unpaid wages and other claims. The chamber’s reversal judgment, adopted by the Assembly of Civil Chambers, refers to Article 44 as the special rule on international jurisdiction in employment matters. The Assembly held that for periods with a valid choice of law, the chosen law of Kosovo or Russia applies; for periods without a choice, Turkish law applies as the more closely connected law, among other reasons because wages were paid into a Turkish bank account and the employee was covered by Turkish social security. The decision shows that a Turkish court having jurisdiction does not mean Turkish law applies.

## Frequently asked questions

### I worked abroad – where can I sue in Turkey?

At the employer’s domicile if it is based in Turkey, and also at the Turkish labour court of your domicile or habitual residence (Article 44 MÖHUK).

### My contract names a foreign court – can I still sue in Turkey?

Yes. The jurisdiction under Article 44 cannot be excluded by contract (Article 47(2) MÖHUK).

### Do I need to go to mediation first if I worked abroad?

Article 3 of Law No. 7036 makes no exception, but whether it applies to cases with a foreign element is disputed. The safe course is to apply for mediation before suing.
