Article 53 of Turkey’s Law No. 5718 on Private International Law and International Civil Procedure (Milletlerarası Özel Hukuk ve Usul Hukuku Hakkında Kanun, MÖHUK; Official Gazette of 12 December 2007, No. 26728) lists the documents that must accompany an application to enforce a foreign judgment (tenfiz). There are two: the judgment in certified form and certified proof that it has become final, each with a certified Turkish translation. If you want a German or Dutch divorce decree or other judgment recognised (tanıma) or enforced in Turkey, you need to get the certificate of finality and the apostille in the country where the judgment was given.

## Article 53 MÖHUK text

Unofficial translation:

> **Documents to be attached to the application**
>
> Article 53 – (1) The following documents shall be attached to the application for enforcement:
>
> a) The original of the foreign court judgment, duly certified by the authorities of that country, or a copy certified by the judicial body that gave the judgment, together with its certified translation.
>
> b) A letter or document showing that the judgment has become final, duly certified by the authorities of that country, together with its certified translation.

Original Turkish text:

> **Dilekçeye eklenecek belgeler**
>
> MADDE 53 – (1) Tenfiz dilekçesine aşağıdaki belgeler eklenir:
>
> a) Yabancı mahkeme ilâmının o ülke makamlarınca usulen onanmış aslı veya ilâmı veren yargı organı tarafından onanmış örneği ve onanmış tercümesi.
>
> b) İlâmın kesinleştiğini gösteren ve o ülke makamlarınca usulen onanmış yazı veya belge ile onanmış tercümesi.

The article has not changed since 2007. As of November 2026, the list of amendments in the current text on UYAP Mevzuat, the Turkish Ministry of Justice’s legislation database, shows only Article 27 (amended by Law No. 7550).

## Legislative reasoning

According to the official reasoning, Article 53 partly corresponds to Article 37 of the former Law No. 2675. The old rule required the certified original only. Because some foreign courts do not release the original, a copy certified by the court that gave the judgment is now also accepted.

## Explanation

### Two documents, two translations

The first document is the judgment itself: the original certified by the authorities of the country of origin, or a copy certified by the court that gave it. The second is proof of finality (kesinleşme belgesi). For German decisions this is normally the note of finality (Rechtskraftvermerk; kesinleşme şerhi) on the court copy. A certified Turkish translation of both documents must be attached. The law does not say who has to certify the translation.

Whether a judgment is final depends on the law of the country where it was given (Article 50(1) MÖHUK), and so does the form of proof. The 2nd Civil Chamber of the Yargıtay (Court of Cassation) accepted a certificate that no appeal had been lodged against a French divorce judgment as sufficient (see below).

### What does “duly certified” mean?

Official documents issued by foreign authorities count as official documents in Turkey only if the competent authority of the issuing state or the relevant Turkish consulate has certified them; provisions of international treaties to which Turkey is a party on the certification of foreign official documents remain unaffected (Article 224 of the Code of Civil Procedure No. 6100, HMK).

One such treaty is the Hague Convention of 5 October 1961 Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents (the Apostille Convention). According to the Hague Conference status table, it has been in force for Turkey since 29 September 1985, for Germany since 13 February 1966 and for the Netherlands since 8 October 1965. Under Article 1, documents from an authority or official connected with the courts are public documents; under Article 3, the only formality that may be required is an apostille issued by the competent authority of the state where the document originates. The apostille certifies the authenticity of the signature, the capacity in which the signer acted and the identity of the seal or stamp (Article 5). It does not confirm the content of the judgment.

### If documents are missing

The Yargıtay treats the documents under Article 53 as a procedural requirement (dava şartı). Missing documents do not lead to immediate dismissal; the court must give the applicant time to file them. If the judgment was final before the case was filed, a note of finality obtained later can be filed during the proceedings (Assembly of Civil Chambers, below). A peremptory time limit (kesin süre) is different: if the apostille is not filed in time, the case may be dismissed.

The same documents are required when only recognition is sought. Article 58 MÖHUK makes recognition depend on a finding that the judgment meets the conditions for enforcement, and two of the decisions below apply Article 53 in recognition cases. For the competent court see [Article 51 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-51-mohuk-competent-court-turkey); for the general framework see [Article 50 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-50-mohuk-enforcement-turkey).

## Practical points if you live abroad

**Complete the documents in the country of origin.** The note of finality and the apostille can only be obtained in the country where the judgment was given. Ask the German court that gave the judgment for a copy bearing the note of finality, then have the competent authority add an apostille. Make sure the apostille covers both the copy of the judgment and the note of finality or separate certificate of finality; Article 53 requires both documents to be certified.

**Have the apostille translated too.** In the 11th Civil Chamber case below, the regional court of appeal relied on the fact that the apostille and its translation were missing, and the Yargıtay upheld that decision. Give the judgment, the note of finality and the apostille to the translator together.

**A photocopy is not enough.** In a recognition case concerning a German divorce judgment, only a photocopy of the translation had been filed; the Yargıtay held that the applicant should have been given time to file the certified documents.

**Service affects finality.** If the other party lived in Turkey when the judgment was given, how it was served on them matters. According to the Assembly of Civil Chambers, Turkey and Germany have both objected to Article 10 of the 1965 Hague Service Convention (direct service by post); sending the judgment directly by post to a person in Turkey is not valid service, and the judgment cannot then be regarded as final under German law. This objection can be raised even if you hold a note of finality.

For general information see [Private international law](/en/practice-areas/private-international-law).

## Yargıtay decisions

**Yargıtay Hukuk Genel Kurulu, E. 2019/272, K. 2019/1396, T. 19.12.2019**

Enforcement of a Cologne judgment was sought; the copy filed with the application had no note of finality, which was obtained during the proceedings. The Assembly of Civil Chambers held that the proof under Article 53(b) must be attached to the application, but the defect could be cured during the proceedings because the judgment had become final before the case was filed; the note of finality is not listed as a condition of enforcement in Articles 50 and 54. What matters is when the judgment actually became final under German law, not a date entered in the note by mistake.

**Yargıtay 2. Hukuk Dairesi, E. 2023/7402, K. 2024/1459, T. 04.03.2024**

In a case to recognise a divorce judgment from Pforzheim, Germany, the applicant had filed only a photocopy of the translation. The Chamber reversed: judgment without giving the applicant time to file the certified documents under Article 53 was an incomplete examination.

**Yargıtay 2. Hukuk Dairesi, E. 2024/3459, K. 2024/9890, T. 12.12.2024**

The first-instance court had refused to treat a certificate that no appeal was lodged against a French divorce judgment as proof of finality. The Chamber reversed: finality and its proof are governed by the procedural law of the country of origin; the certificate showed finality, and no further document was needed.

**Yargıtay 11. Hukuk Dairesi, E. 2025/1013, K. 2025/5575, T. 22.09.2025**

In a case to enforce a Swiss judgment, the court had set a peremptory time limit of 60 days for the apostille. The apostille was issued and filed only after the limit had expired. The Chamber upheld the dismissal.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which documents are needed to recognise a foreign divorce in Turkey?

A certified copy of the judgment, certified proof of finality and certified Turkish translations of both (Article 53 MÖHUK). For German and Dutch documents, certification is by apostille.

### What happens if the certificate of finality is missing?

The court sets a time limit to file it. If the judgment was final before the case was filed, filing it during the proceedings is sufficient (Assembly of Civil Chambers, 19 December 2019). Missing a peremptory time limit can lead to dismissal.

### Do these requirements also apply to recognition only?

Yes. Article 58 MÖHUK refers to the conditions for enforcement, and the Yargıtay requires the Article 53 documents in recognition cases as well.
