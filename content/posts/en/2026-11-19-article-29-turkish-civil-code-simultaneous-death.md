Article 29 of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721 (Türk Medenî Kanunu, TMK) puts the burden of proof on whoever claims a right based on a person being alive or dead. If it cannot be proved which of several people died first, they are all deemed to have died at the same time (presumption of simultaneous death, birlikte ölüm karinesi, often called commorientes). When family members die in the same accident or disaster, this presumption decides who inherits. For heirs living in Germany or the Netherlands, the question usually comes up when the estate in Türkiye is divided.

## Article 29 TMK – text

Unofficial translation:

> **II. Proof of life and death**
>
> **1. Burden of proof**
>
> Article 29 – Anyone who, in order to exercise a right, claims that a person is alive or dead, or was alive at a certain time or at the death of another person, must prove that claim.
>
> If it cannot be proved which of several persons died before or after the other, they are all deemed to have died at the same time.

Turkish original:

> **II. Sağ olmanın ve ölümün ispatı**
>
> **1. İspat yükü**
>
> Madde 29- Bir hakkın kullanılması için bir kimsenin sağ veya ölü olduğunu veya belirli bir zamanda ya da başka bir kimsenin ölümünde sağ bulunduğunu ileri süren kimse, iddiasını ispat etmek zorundadır.
>
> Birden fazla kişiden hangisinin önce veya sonra öldüğü ispat edilemezse, hepsi aynı anda ölmüş sayılır.

The article has not changed since the Code entered into force on 1 January 2002. It does not appear in the amendment table of the current text on mevzuat.gov.tr; the latest law in that table is Law No. 7589 (Articles 440 and 444, in force since 31 October 2026).

## Legislative reasoning

According to the reasoning, the article corresponds to Article 28 of the former Code. The heading was modernised ("Sağ olmanın ve ölümün ispatı" instead of "Sağlığın ve ölümün ispatı"), and so was the side heading ("İspat yükü" instead of "Beyyine külfeti") (source: reasoning of the government bill submitted to the Grand National Assembly). The reasoning mentions no change in substance.

## Explanation

### Who has to prove what

Paragraph 1 applies the general burden-of-proof rule in Article 6 TMK to the facts of life and death. The procedural counterpart is Article 190(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure No. 6100 (Hukuk Muhakemeleri Kanunu, HMK): the burden lies on the party who derives a favourable legal consequence from the alleged fact. So the first thing to ask is who benefits from a particular order of death. Whoever claims a share of an estate on the basis of that order has to prove it.

The order of death is a fact, not a legal transaction. The rule in Article 200 HMK requiring proof by documents therefore does not apply, and the court weighs hospital records, accident and autopsy reports and witness evidence together. If the civil registry shows two deaths on the same date, that alone does not show who died first.

### The presumption and rebuttal

Paragraph 2 is a statutory presumption. Under Article 190(2) HMK, a party relying on a statutory presumption only has to prove the fact it rests on: here, that several people died and the order cannot be established. The other side may rebut the presumption unless the law makes an exception. There is no such exception for Article 29(2) TMK; once the order is proved, the presumption does not apply.

Do not confuse it with the presumption of death in Article 31 TMK. Article 31 answers whether someone whose body was never found is treated as dead. Article 29(2) deals with the order of deaths that are not in doubt.

### Effect on inheritance: a mother and her son

To inherit, a person must be alive and capable of inheriting when the deceased dies (Article 580(1) TMK). If an heir alive at that moment dies later, the inheritance right passes to the heir's own heirs (Article 580(2)). A difference of a few minutes can therefore decide which family receives an estate.

Suppose a mother and her married son, who has children of his own, die in the same accident. If the son outlived his mother, he first inherits from her. Her share then forms part of his estate and passes to his wife and children; the wife takes one quarter alongside descendants (Article 499(1) TMK). If the son died before his mother, or both are deemed to have died together, he does not inherit from her. His share of her estate goes to his descendants (Article 495(3)), and his wife receives nothing from her mother-in-law's estate, not even indirectly. If the son had no children, the mother's estate goes to her other heirs.

Article 495(3) TMK speaks of descendants taking the place of children who "died before the deceased". The Code has no express rule for a child deemed to have died at the same time. In the author's assessment, the descendants take that child's place as well, because the child was not alive when the estate opened (Article 580(1)); a court may take a different view in a particular case.

The same logic applies to legacies: to receive a legacy, the beneficiary must be alive when the testator dies (Article 581(1)). A beneficiary deemed to have died together with the testator does not meet this condition.

## What this means in practice if you live abroad

### An accident abroad

When a Turkish citizen dies abroad, the death is entered in the Turkish civil registry through the consulate or embassy, which must report it within ten days of learning of it (Article 31(1) of the Civil Registry Services Act No. 5490). The evidence on the order of death, however, is usually in the country where the accident happened: hospital files, rescue service reports, police or prosecution files. For a Turkish court to treat foreign public documents as official, they need certification (Article 224 HMK; apostille) and a translation (Article 223 HMK). It is easier to request these documents soon after the accident than to track them down later; this is the author's suggestion.

### Which law applies

Succession is governed by the national law of the deceased; Turkish law always applies to immovable property in Türkiye (Article 20(1) of the Turkish Private International Law Act No. 5718, MÖHUK). Where Turkish spouses, or a Turkish parent and child, die together, a Turkish court decides the order under Article 29(2) TMK. If family members have different nationalities, for example one spouse holds only German or Dutch citizenship, a different law may govern each estate.

This point is disputed. MÖHUK does not say expressly whether the order of death and the presumption of simultaneous death fall under the law governing succession (Article 20 MÖHUK) or under the national law governing legal capacity (Article 9(1) MÖHUK), and no binding court decision on it was reviewed for this article. Different legal systems may have different rules for cases where the order of death cannot be established, and two laws can lead to conflicting results. Which approach a court will take in a given case is uncertain.

German or Dutch courts and authorities determine the applicable law under their own conflict rules and may reach a different law. For the rules of German or Dutch law, please contact a lawyer or notary admitted in that country. More on the law governing succession: [Article 20 MÖHUK – Inheritance law in Turkey](/en/publications/legislation/article-20-mohuk-inheritance-turkey).

## Frequently asked questions

### Who decides on the presumption of simultaneous death?

The presumption follows from the Code; no separate decision is needed. If the order of death is disputed, the court examines in the certificate of inheritance or estate division proceedings whether it has been proved. How to obtain a certificate of inheritance: [Certificate of inheritance in Turkey](/en/publications/certificate-of-inheritance-turkey).

### Do spouses who die together inherit from each other?

No. Neither was alive when the other died (Article 580(1) TMK); each estate goes to that spouse's own statutory heirs.

### Can the presumption be rebutted?

Yes. A party who proves the order of death rebuts the presumption (Article 190(2) HMK).
