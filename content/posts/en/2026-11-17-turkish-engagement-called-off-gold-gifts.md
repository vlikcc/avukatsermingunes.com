When an engagement is called off, Turkish law lets the person who gave an out-of-the-ordinary gift, such as gold, valuable jewellery or a large sum of money, claim it back from the other fiancé(e); gifts from the parents are covered too (Article 122 of the Turkish Civil Code No. 4721, TMK). It does not matter who ended the engagement. Damages and compensation for distress, on the other hand, can only be claimed from the party at fault (Articles 120 and 121 TMK), and all these claims become time-barred one year after the engagement ends (Article 123 TMK).

This article covers the return of gifts, compensation for financial loss and for distress, the time limit, and the competent court in Turkey. As of November 2026, Articles 118 to 123 TMK are unchanged; the latest amending law in the amendment table is Law No. 7589, which does not touch these articles.

## Engagement (nişanlılık) under Turkish law: can you sue to make someone marry?

An engagement (nişanlanma) is formed by a promise to marry (Article 118 TMK). The law does not require a ceremony, rings or a written document. A minor or a person under guardianship is not bound without the consent of their legal representative. Whether the families call it "söz", "nişan" or "söz kesme" makes no difference in my reading of the law; what counts is a serious, mutual promise to marry.

An engagement gives no right to sue for marriage (Article 119(1) TMK). A penalty or forfeit agreed in case one side backs out cannot be enforced in court, but money already paid under such an agreement cannot be reclaimed either (Article 119(2) TMK). After a break-up, three kinds of claim remain: return of gifts, damages, and compensation for distress.

## Engagement called off: who keeps the gold and the gifts?

If the engagement ends for any reason other than marriage, out-of-the-ordinary gifts can be reclaimed (Article 122 TMK):

- **Who can claim?** The giver: the fiancé(e), their parents, or people who acted as parents.
- **From whom?** The other fiancé(e), who received the gift.
- **Which gifts?** Only "out-of-the-ordinary" ones (alışılmışın dışındaki). The law does not define the term. Gold, jewellery (ziynet), a valuable watch or a large sum of money qualify; flowers or small everyday presents do not. In my view the line depends on the parties' financial situation and local custom.
- **Does fault matter?** No. Unlike Articles 120 and 121, Article 122 does not require fault. The person who broke off the engagement can also reclaim their gifts.
- **Which endings?** Any ending other than marriage, including the death of a fiancé(e) or a declaration of absence (official reasoning of the article).

The gift is returned as it is. If that is not possible, an equivalent item of the same kind and quality must be given; only if that is impossible too do the rules on unjust enrichment apply (Article 122(2) TMK). According to the official reasoning, giving priority to an equivalent item is meant to protect the giver against losses from inflation. In my view, this means that for a gold bangle that has been sold, a bangle of the same carat and weight can be claimed first.

### Disputed points: guests' gold and the engagement ring

These questions are disputed. What follows concerns points the law does not expressly settle and reflects my own assessment; no binding court decision is cited in this article. How a court would decide a particular case is uncertain.

- **Gold pinned on at the engagement party by relatives and guests.** Article 122 TMK lists only gifts from the engaged couple and from parents or people acting as parents. Gold from uncles, aunts or guests is not on that list, so the wording does not say who owns it or who can reclaim it. I think local custom and the person it was pinned on are what matter.
- **Engagement ring.** A valuable ring can be an out-of-the-ordinary gift; a ring of little value may not be.
- **Engagement gift or wedding gold?** If the engagement ends in marriage, Article 122 does not apply. Who keeps gold given at the wedding or during the marriage depends on other rules and on the case law of the Court of Cassation (Yargıtay); see [Turkish wedding gold after divorce: who owns it?](/en/publications/turkish-wedding-gold-divorce-who-owns-it). In my view the same rules, not Article 122, apply to gold given at the engagement that stayed with a spouse after the wedding, because in that case the engagement ended in marriage.

## Damages after a broken engagement

If one fiancé(e) ends the engagement without good reason, or it ends for a reason attributable to one side, the party at fault must pay the other appropriate compensation (Article 120(1) TMK). It covers:

- expenses the other party incurred with a view to the marriage,
- other financial sacrifices made with a view to the marriage,
- the costs of the engagement.

The entitled party's parents, or people who acted as parents, can claim their own expenses on the same terms (Article 120(2) TMK). The law speaks of "appropriate" compensation in line with good faith, so not every expense is reimbursed in full. Examples are a deposit for a wedding hall, furniture bought for the new home, or giving up a job because of the planned marriage.

Fault is required here. Someone who ends the engagement for good reason pays nothing; the person whose fault caused the break-up may have to pay.

## Compensation for distress (manevi tazminat)

A party whose personality rights are violated as a result of the break-up can claim an appropriate sum of money from the other party if that party is at fault (Article 121 TMK). Both conditions must be met: a violation of personality rights and fault on the other side. The break-up alone is not enough. An example would be an engagement ended in a way that humiliates the person in their community, or defamatory claims spread when it was broken off. The official reasoning says the old requirement of "serious" harm was deliberately dropped, and compensation is awarded only in money.

The claim cannot be assigned unless the other side accepts it, and passes to the heirs only if the entitled person had already asserted it (Article 25(4) TMK).

## Time limit: one year from the end of the engagement

Claims arising from the end of an engagement become time-barred **one year** after the engagement ends (Article 123 TMK). This applies equally to the return of gifts, damages and compensation for distress.

The period runs from the end of the engagement, not from when someone learns of it. The official reasoning says so expressly: nobody should be able to sue years later by claiming they found out late. If the date of the break-up is disputed, messages about it or the date the rings were returned become important evidence.

## Which court in Turkey, and is mediation required?

Family courts (aile mahkemesi) hear cases arising from family law under the Second Book of the TMK, except its Third Part (Article 4, item 1 of Law No. 4787 on Family Courts). The engagement provisions are in the First Part of the Second Book, so these cases go to the family court. Where no family court has been set up, a civil court of first instance (asliye hukuk mahkemesi) designated by the Council of Judges and Prosecutors hears them (Article 2(2) of Law No. 4787).

There is no special rule on territorial jurisdiction for engagement claims. The general rule is that the court where the defendant lives when the case is filed has jurisdiction (Article 6(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure No. 6100, HMK).

As of November 2026, Article 18/B of Law No. 6325 on Mediation in Civil Disputes (HUAK) lists the cases in which mediation is a precondition for filing suit: tenancy, partition, condominium and neighbour disputes. Disputes over a broken engagement are not on the list. Voluntary mediation is available for disputes the parties can freely settle, except those involving allegations of domestic violence (Article 1(2) HUAK). I consider claims for return of gifts and for money suitable for voluntary mediation; this is my assessment.

## If you live in Germany or the Netherlands

If one fiancé(e) is a foreign national or the engagement took place abroad, Article 12 of the Turkish Private International Law Act No. 5718 (MÖHUK) determines the applicable law; this is covered in [Article 12 MÖHUK – Engagement to Marry](/en/publications/legislation/article-12-mohuk-engagement). In short: if both are Turkish citizens, a Turkish court applies Turkish law; if they have different nationalities, it also applies Turkish law.

If the defendant lives in Germany or the Netherlands and has no residence in Turkey, the law does not expressly say which Turkish court has jurisdiction. The international jurisdiction of Turkish courts follows the domestic rules on territorial jurisdiction (Article 40 MÖHUK). Article 41 MÖHUK contains a fallback rule for personal status cases of Turkish citizens, but whether engagement claims fall under it is disputed. If a claim is brought in Germany or the Netherlands, that court applies its own rules; please consult a lawyer qualified there. ## Frequently asked questions

### Engagement called off: who keeps the gold?

Gold and valuable jewellery given by the engaged couple or their parents can be reclaimed by the giver, whoever ended the engagement (Article 122 TMK). The law has no express rule on gold given by guests.

### I broke off the engagement. Can I get my gifts back?

Yes, reclaiming gifts does not depend on fault. If you had no good reason, however, the other side may claim damages and compensation for distress (Articles 120 and 121 TMK).

### How long do I have to sue after a broken engagement in Turkey?

One year from the end of the engagement (Article 123 TMK).

### Which court in Turkey hears broken engagement cases?

The family court (Article 4, item 1 of Law No. 4787), as a rule where the defendant lives (Article 6(1) HMK).
