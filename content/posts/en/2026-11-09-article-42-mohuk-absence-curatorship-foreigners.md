Article 42 of Turkey's Law No. 5718 on Private International Law and International Civil Procedure (MÖHUK) decides which Turkish court rules on guardianship (vesayet), curatorship (kayyımlık), restriction of capacity (kısıtlılık), declaration of absence (gaiplik) and declaration of death (ölmüş sayılma) for a foreigner who has no domicile in Turkey. The court of the place where the person is staying in Turkey has jurisdiction; if the person is not in Turkey, the court where their property is located. In practice this matters most to former Turkish citizens who took German or Dutch citizenship and still own land or an inheritance share in Turkey.

## Article 42 MÖHUK text

Unofficial translation:

> **Certain proceedings concerning the personal status of foreigners**
>
> Article 42 – (1) Decisions on guardianship, curatorship, restriction of capacity, absence and declaration of death concerning a foreigner who has no domicile in Turkey are given by the court of the place where the person concerned resides in Turkey or, if he does not reside there, by the court of the place where his property is located.

Original Turkish text:

> **Yabancıların kişi hâllerine ilişkin bazı davalar**
>
> MADDE 42 – (1) Türkiye'de yerleşim yeri bulunmayan yabancı hakkında vesâyet, kayyımlık, kısıtlılık, gaiplik ve ölmüş sayılma kararları ilgilinin Türkiye'de sâkin olduğu yer, sâkin değilse mallarının bulunduğu yer mahkemesince verilir.

The article has not changed since the Law entered into force on 12 December 2007; as of November 2026 the current text in UYAP Mevzuat carries no amendment note for it.

## Legislative reasoning

According to the reasoning of the draft law, the article partly corresponds to Article 29 of the former Law No. 2675. The rule itself was not changed; only the terms were aligned with the Turkish Civil Code, replacing “ikametgâh” with “yerleşim yeri” (domicile) and “hacir” with “kısıtlılık” (restriction of capacity). (Source: reasoning of the draft law submitted to the Grand National Assembly, Article 41 of the draft.)

## Explanation

Two conditions apply: the person is a foreigner, and has no domicile in Turkey. The court first looks at where the person is actually staying in Turkey. If they are not in Turkey, the court where their property is located has jurisdiction.

Article 42 only settles which court decides. The applicable law is set out elsewhere. The grounds for guardianship and restriction of capacity are governed by the person's national law, while all other questions of guardianship and restriction, and curatorship, are governed by Turkish law ([Article 10 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-10-mohuk-guardianship-turkey)). Absence and declaration of death are also governed by national law; if that law does not allow such a decision, Turkish law applies where the person has property in Turkey or the spouse or one of the heirs is a Turkish citizen ([Article 11 MÖHUK](/en/publications/legislation/article-11-mohuk-declaration-of-absence)).

The competent court is usually the civil court of peace (sulh hukuk mahkemesi). It acts as the guardianship authority (Turkish Civil Code, TMK, Art. 397(2)). A declaration of absence is a non-contentious matter (çekişmesiz yargı), and unless a rule says otherwise these go to the same court (Code of Civil Procedure, HMK, Art. 382(2)(a)(4) and Art. 383(1)).

Article 42 does not apply to Turkish citizens; the Civil Code's rules do. For absence, for example, the court of the last domicile in Turkey decides, or, if the person never lived in Turkey, the court of the place where they are entered in the civil registry (nüfus) (TMK Art. 32(2)).

## Practical points for people living abroad

### Mavi Kart holders count as foreigners here

If you gave up Turkish citizenship with permission (çıkma izni) and became German or Dutch, Article 28 of the Turkish Citizenship Law No. 5901 lets you keep the rights of Turkish citizens, apart from the exceptions it lists. It grants rights, not citizenship, and MÖHUK has no rule treating Mavi Kart holders as Turkish. In my assessment, therefore, if a Mavi Kart holder has no domicile in Turkey, jurisdiction follows Article 42 and the applicable law under Articles 10 and 11 follows the new citizenship. A dual citizen who kept Turkish citizenship remains Turkish; the Civil Code rules (such as TMK Art. 32(2)) and Article 4(1)(b) MÖHUK apply. More on the card: [Blue Card Turkey (Mavi Kart)](/en/publications/blue-card-turkey-mavi-kart-rights).

### A curator for property in Turkey

If someone has been missing for a long time and their place of residence is unknown, the guardianship authority appoints a management curator (yönetim kayyımı) for their property (TMK Art. 427(1)(1)). Because curatorship is governed by Turkish law (Article 10(3) MÖHUK), this also applies to the inheritance share of a German or Dutch citizen in Turkey. If that person is not in Turkey, the application goes to the civil court of peace where the property is (Article 42 MÖHUK); TMK Art. 430(2) also points to the place of the property for a management curator.

### Declaration of absence

The Turkish court first applies the person's national law, for a German citizen German law (Article 11 MÖHUK). For the content of that law, please consult a lawyer admitted in that country. Where Turkish law applies, at least one year must have passed since the danger of death or at least five years since the last news (TMK Art. 33(1)).

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Turkish court declares a foreigner absent?

Without a domicile in Turkey, the court where the person is staying in Turkey; otherwise the court where the property is located (Article 42 MÖHUK). The civil court of peace hears the case (HMK Art. 383(1)).

### Can a curator be appointed in Turkey for a Mavi Kart holder?

Yes. If they own property in Turkey, the civil court of peace where it is located can appoint a management curator under Turkish law (Articles 10(3) and 42 MÖHUK; TMK Art. 427).

### Does Article 42 MÖHUK apply to dual citizens?

No. A person who kept Turkish citizenship is Turkish, and jurisdiction follows the Civil Code.
